# Working with a marketing agency in another country

More businesses now choose agencies based on fit rather than postcode. Websites, brand identities and campaigns are produced in shared documents and design files, and reviewed on video calls. Distance matters less than it did. What matters more is how the collaboration is set up.

This is a practical guide to what you should agree with an agency abroad before work starts, and what you can expect from a well-run remote project.

## Start with a written scope

The most common source of friction in any agency project is a different understanding of what is included. With a remote agency, a clear written scope matters even more, because there are fewer informal conversations to clear things up.

A good scope answers:

- which pages, materials or campaigns will be delivered
- who writes the copy, and in which languages
- how many rounds of feedback are included
- what you need to provide, and by when
- what happens after launch

If an agency cannot describe the deliverables in plain language before you sign, it will be harder to do so halfway through the project.

## Agree how and when you will communicate

Time zones are rarely the problem people expect. Between Northern Europe and the Gulf, for example, the difference is two to three hours, so most of the working day overlaps. The real question is rhythm.

Agree on:

- one main contact person on each side
- a regular check-in, for example a short weekly call during active phases
- where files, feedback and decisions are kept, so nothing lives only in someone's inbox
- expected response times for questions and approvals

## Make feedback easy to give

Feedback is where projects speed up or stall. Ask the agency how they present work. Designs shown in context, such as a homepage on a phone or a social post in a feed, are easier to judge than isolated files. Comments collected in one place are easier to act on than scattered emails.

Decide internally who has the final say. When several people give feedback, the agency needs one consolidated response, not five different opinions.

## Be clear about language and market

If your audience reads in more than one language, say so at the start. A website planned for English and Arabic, for instance, needs typography, layout and structure that work in both directions. Adding a language later is possible, but it costs more than planning for it.

Ask who writes and reviews each language. Copy in a language your agency does not work in natively should be written or reviewed by a native speaker.

## Ask about ownership and handover

Before you start, confirm:

- that your domain is registered in your company's name
- that you receive the final files, guidelines and, if relevant, the source code
- how hosting and maintenance are handled after launch, and what happens if you move to another supplier later

These questions are not a sign of mistrust. A serious agency will have clear answers.

## What a good remote collaboration feels like

You always know what is happening next. Decisions are written down. Work is presented clearly and on time. Problems are raised early, not at the deadline. The agency recommends what you need, and tells you when something is not necessary.
